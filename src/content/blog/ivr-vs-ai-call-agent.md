You've done it yourself. You ring a company, a recorded voice reads out six options, none of them is quite what you want, and by option four you've forgotten what option two was. You press zero and hope. Sometimes you just hang up.

That's an IVR, the "press 1, press 2" menu most Nepali banks, telecoms and hospitals still use. It was a sensible idea when it arrived. A recorded menu costs far less than a room full of operators, and it sends some calls to the right desk.

The trouble is what it does to callers who don't fit the menu, which is most of them.

## Where menus lose people

A menu only works if the caller already knows which category their question belongs to. "Is the cardiology OPD open on Sunday?" Is that appointments, or general enquiries, or the hospital information line? The caller guesses, waits, and gets transferred.

Menus are also slow. Each level adds fifteen or twenty seconds of listening before anything happens. Two levels deep and the caller has spent most of a minute without a single question answered.

And menus can't answer anything themselves. At best they get the caller to a queue, and the queue is where the line was busy in the first place.

## What an AI call agent does differently

An AI call agent skips the menu. The caller just says what they want, in Nepali, the way they'd say it to a person: "I want to know if Dr. Sharma is seeing patients on Sunday."

The agent answers from what the hospital has given it, the OPD schedule, the doctor rota, the visiting hours. If the question needs a person, such as a test result or a billing dispute, it transfers the call to the right desk with a note of what was asked. Nobody presses anything.

It's the difference between a signpost and a receptionist.

## When a menu is still fine

If your calls really do split cleanly, a menu can be the right tool. A company with a sales line and a support line, and callers who know which they want, doesn't need anything clever. "Press 1 for sales, 2 for support" is quick and clear.
