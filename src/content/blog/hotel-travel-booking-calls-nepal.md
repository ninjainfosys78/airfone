A guest in Pokhara rings a lakeside hotel at ten at night. The receptionist is helping a family check in, with three suitcases on the floor and a child asleep on a sofa, so the phone goes unanswered. The guest on the other end has already found two other hotels on the map and has the numbers open. The next one picks up, and that is where they stay.

Hotels, lodges and travel agencies in Nepal live on the phone more than most businesses. Trekkers want to know about permits and whether a porter can be arranged. Families want a room near the lake with a view. Someone in Delhi asks about a Pashupatinath tour for six people. A tour operator in Kathmandu wants to confirm a group arriving on the afternoon flight. And the call can come in at any hour, from any time zone, in any of three languages.

This guide is about that problem: what a booking call is really made of, why the language mix makes it harder than it looks, and what an [AI call agent](/products/ai-call-agent) can and cannot do on a hotel or travel agency line.

## Three languages, sometimes in one sentence

The caller you get on a Nepali tourism line does not stick to one language. A Nepali family from Kathmandu says "room available cha?" and expects you to follow. A traveller from Bihar asks in Hindi whether breakfast is included and whether there is parking for a car. A guest from Europe speaks English with a few Nepali words they picked up on the way, and says "namaste" before every sentence.

Your best receptionist handles all of this without thinking. She hears the first few words, picks the right language, and carries on. The problem is not skill. It is that she is one person, she is only there for part of the day, and during the busy weeks she is also checking people in, answering the door and sorting out a bill.

An AI call agent answers your normal number and speaks Nepali, English and Hindi. It replies in the language the caller starts with and stays in it. One English word in a Nepali sentence does not make it switch, which matters because that is how most Nepali callers talk. If the caller moves into whole sentences in another language, or asks for one, it follows them. How well it copes with real accents, background noise and mixed speech is covered in [Can an AI really speak Nepali on the phone?](/blog/nepali-voice-ai).

It does not claim to speak every language. If a caller speaks something else entirely, the right behaviour is to say so politely and pass the call to a person, not to guess.

## What people actually ask

Most booking calls are the same handful of questions, asked over and over by different people:

**For a hotel or lodge.** Is there a room on these dates, and what does it cost per night? Is breakfast included? Is there pickup from the airport or the bus park, and how much? How far is it from the lake, the temple or the trek start? Is there hot water, wifi, parking? What is the cancellation rule if plans change?

**For a travel agency or tour operator.** What is in the package and what is not? What is the price per person, and is it lower for a group? When does the next group leave? Do I need a permit, and do you arrange it? Can the itinerary be shortened by a day?

Almost all of these have one right answer, and that answer already lives in your rate card, your package sheet or the notes the manager keeps in a drawer. You give the agent that information once. When rates change for the season, you change them once, and the next caller hears the new price. There is nothing to re-record and nothing to retrain.

## Seasons change the questions

A hotel in Nepal does not have a steady year. The autumn trekking season, Dashain and Tihar, the spring rush and the monsoon lull each bring a different caller with a different question.

In peak weeks the phone never stops, and most calls are for dates you are already full on. A person spends the day saying "sorry, full" forty times. The agent can say it for them, offer the nearest free dates, and take a name for a waiting list, so your staff only deal with calls that can turn into a booking.

In the quiet months the problem reverses. There are fewer calls, and every one matters, so a missed one hurts more. The agent makes sure none of them goes unanswered while your small team is cleaning rooms or out on errands.

## Taking the booking details without losing them

A phone booking usually fails in the handover. The caller says dates and numbers quickly, the receptionist writes them on a scrap of paper, and by the time someone calls back a name is spelled wrong or a date has been written for the wrong month.

The agent asks for each detail in turn: check-in and check-out dates, number of adults and children, room type or destination, the caller's name and a number to reach them on. It repeats them back to the caller so mistakes are caught on the call, not three days later. Then it passes the whole request to your team.

Every call is recorded and written out as a transcript, so when your staff ring back to confirm, they are reading what the guest actually said, not a note someone scribbled. If a guest later says they were quoted a different price, you can check what was said.

Confirmation stays with you. The agent does not promise a room that you have not agreed to hold, and it does not take payment on the call. It collects what a person needs to say yes.

## Groups, agents and the calls that are not simple

Not every call is a single guest with a single question. A tour operator ringing to place twelve people over three nights has questions about rooming lists, a late arrival, one vegetarian and a guide who needs a bed. A returning guest wants the same room as last year. A family wants to know if a grandmother who cannot climb stairs can be on the ground floor.

These are the calls where a human should be on the line, and the agent should recognise that quickly. It handles the opening, takes the key facts and passes the call along with a short note, so the person who picks up already knows who is calling and what they want. Nobody has to start from the beginning.

## Where it hands over to a person

Some calls should never be answered by software. A group asking for a negotiated rate. A guest with a complaint about last night's stay. A caller who is upset because a flight was cancelled and they will arrive a day late. Someone asking about a refund.

The agent recognises these, tells the caller that a person will help, and transfers the call along with what was said. If nobody is free to take it, it records the request and your team sees it as the first thing in the morning.

You set the rules. Some properties send every price question to a person. Others let the agent quote the published rate and only hand over discounts. Some route anything involving money to the owner. Whatever you choose is written once and followed on every call.

## The calls that arrive while you are asleep

Travel enquiries come from other time zones. A family in the UK calls in their afternoon, which is the middle of the night in Nepal. A traveller in Dubai calls after work. A phone that rings out is a booking that goes to the next name on the list.

Because the agent answers at any hour, those callers get their answer, leave their details and are in your list when the team starts in the morning. Compare that to the usual alternative: a voicemail nobody checks until noon, by which time the caller has booked somewhere else.

For arrivals, the same system can ring guests ahead of time to confirm the pickup time or ask which flight they are on, so your driver is not waiting at the wrong gate.

## What it changes for the people at the desk

The receptionist does not disappear, and nobody should pretend that a phone agent can welcome a tired guest at the door. The difference is what she spends her day on.

Instead of answering the same question on rates and distance forty times, she looks after the guests in front of her, deals with the upset caller who needs a person, and follows up the enquiries that are worth a call back. The routine questions are already handled, and every one of them is written down.

For the guest, it simply means the phone gets answered, in a language they are comfortable in, at an hour that suits them.

## Where to start

Most properties begin with after-hours calls and the busy season, then widen it once they have listened to a few weeks of calls and are comfortable with the answers. You keep your existing number, so guests notice only that someone picks up.

A sensible first week looks like this. Put your rates, room types, package details, cancellation rule and directions into the agent. Decide which calls always go to a person. Switch it on for evenings and nights only. Read the transcripts each morning for a week and fix anything it got wrong or answered too loosely. Then extend it to the hours when you are busiest.
