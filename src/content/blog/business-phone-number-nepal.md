A few years ago, a hardware shop on New Road moved to a bigger place in Kalimati. New shutter, new signboard, three times the floor space. What nobody thought about until the week of the move was the phone number.

It was printed on the old signboard, on every invoice book, on the side of the delivery van and in hundreds of contacts saved as "Hardware dai". It was also the owner's personal mobile, and his son had been talking about switching to a different SIM for the data plan.

They kept the number in the end. But it was close, and it made them realise something most businesses only notice when it is almost too late: the phone number is one of the most valuable things a small business owns, and almost nobody treats it that way.

## Why your business phone number matters more than the phone

Customers do not remember your website. They remember the number on the board, the one on last year's bill, the one their cousin passed on. A number that has been in use for years carries every one of those saved contacts with it.

Change it and some of those customers are simply gone. They call the old number, hear that it is out of service, and call someone else. There is no error message on your side. The calls just stop.

So the first rule is simple. Pick a number you are willing to keep for a very long time, and set it up so that it can stay yours whatever else changes.

## Whose business phone number is it?

The most common problem we see is not the type of number. It is who it belongs to.

In a lot of businesses the "business number" is the owner's own mobile. That works while the business is one person. It starts to break the day there is a second person who should answer, a holiday, a shift, or a family member who takes over.

A business number should belong to the business. It should be able to ring whoever is on duty, not one handset in one pocket. And if a staff member leaves, the number should stay behind with the business, not walk out with them.

## Mobile or landline?

Both are fine, and the answer is less important than people think.

A mobile number is easy to get and easy for customers to save. A landline number looks established on an invoice, and some customers still trust it more. What decides whether either one works well is not the number itself but what happens when someone dials it.

If it rings one phone on one desk, you will miss calls whenever that person is busy, out or on another call. If it runs through a phone system, the same number can ring a whole team, wait in a queue during the rush, follow your opening hours, and be answered by an [AI call agent](/products/ai-call-agent) when nobody is free.

## Keeping your number when you change things

Most of the moments that put a number at risk are good news: a move, a second branch, a bigger team, a new provider with better rates.

The way to protect the number through all of them is to stop tying it to a physical line on a particular desk. When your existing number connects to an online phone system through what is called a SIP line, the number no longer depends on where your office is or which handset is plugged in. You can move across the city, open a branch in another town, or let staff answer from the app on their phones, and customers keep dialling exactly the same digits.

We wrote about the system side of this, and when it is worth switching, in [IP/PBX or a traditional phone line](/blog/ip-pbx-vs-phone-line). Here the point is narrower. Whatever system you use, the number should be able to move with you.

## One number or several?

Most small businesses need one main number and should guard it carefully. As you grow, a few extra numbers can be useful:

A branch line, so a Pokhara branch has its own number but still sits on the same system as head office.

A campaign number, printed on one advert or one flyer, so you can see how many calls that campaign actually brought in.

A support line, separate from sales, so a customer with a fault does not wait behind someone asking for a price.

The main number should still be the one on your signboard. The others sit around it.

## What to ask before you choose a business phone number

Whether you are getting a new number or moving an old one, five questions save a lot of trouble later.

Does the number stay mine if I leave? Can it ring more than one phone, and more than one person? Can I change opening hours, menus and who answers myself, without waiting for a technician? Are calls recorded, so I can check what was said? And what happens to a call when nobody is free to answer?

If the answer to the last one is "it rings out", that is the next thing to fix.
