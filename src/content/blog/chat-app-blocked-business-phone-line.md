In the first week of September 2025, a lot of Nepali businesses found that their customer service had disappeared.

Nothing was wrong with their staff, their computers or their internet. But the government had blocked 26 social media and messaging platforms that had not registered under new rules: Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, YouTube and others. For a few days they could not be reached in the ordinary way from inside Nepal.

What followed is now part of the country's history. Young people came out in very large numbers. Many were killed. The block was lifted within days, and the government fell. Those events are far bigger than anything a business blog should try to explain, and we are not going to try.

This post is about something much smaller, which every business owner who lived through that week noticed. For a few days, the channel many of them had built their whole customer contact on stopped working, and they had no say in it.

## What actually stopped

Think about how a typical small business in Kathmandu, Pokhara or Biratnagar handled customers in August 2025.

A clothing shop took orders through Instagram messages and a Facebook page. A tuition centre ran its parent groups on WhatsApp. A travel agency answered every booking question on WhatsApp, because tourists abroad preferred it. A restaurant took table bookings in Messenger. A clinic told patients to "just WhatsApp the reception".

None of these were bad decisions. Customers like chat apps. They are free, fast, and already on everyone's phone. Many businesses grew because they met customers where customers already were.

But when the block came, every one of those conversations went quiet at the same moment. Orders in progress could not be confirmed. Parents could not get school notices. Tourists already on their way could not reach the agency that had their booking. Some people found ways round the block, others did not, and a business cannot plan on its customers knowing how to get round a block.

The businesses that coped best that week had something boring in common. **Their customers had their phone number, and the number worked.**

## The lesson is not "chat apps are bad"

It would be easy to draw the wrong conclusion. Chat apps are not going away, and they are not bad for business. Viber was one of the few platforms that had registered before the deadline and stayed available. WhatsApp and the others came back within days. Most businesses went straight back to using them, and that is fine.

The real lesson is about **who controls the channel.**

When your customers reach you through a platform, the platform's owner decides whether that channel exists, what it costs, what you are allowed to send, and whether your account stays open. So can the country's rules about that platform. Those decisions can change overnight, and none of them is yours.

That is true of any platform, anywhere, not just of one September in Nepal. A business account can be banned by mistake. A messaging price can go up. A platform can change how business messages work, or stop offering a feature you rely on. A country can decide a platform must register, and the platform can decide not to.

So the question every business should ask is not "which app should we use?" It is: **if any single app stopped working tomorrow, could our customers still reach us?**

## Why the phone number is different

A phone number is the oldest customer channel there is, and in this one respect it is the strongest.

**It works on every phone.** A smartphone, a basic keypad phone, a landline in a government office. No app to install, no account to create, no update to download.

**It needs no data.** A normal phone call works with no mobile data at all. For a farmer with a basic phone, a grandmother with no data pack, or anyone in an area where data is weak but the voice signal holds, a call works when an app does not.

**It is governed here, by licensed operators.** Phone numbers in Nepal are issued and carried by telecom operators licensed in the country under the Nepal Telecommunications Authority. They are not a foreign platform's account that can be suspended by an automated system on another continent.

**Everyone knows how to use it.** Nobody needs to be taught how to dial a number. Your oldest customer and your newest one both can.

**It is printed everywhere already.** It is on your signboard, your invoices, your vehicle, your Google listing and in hundreds of customers' contact lists. That is years of accumulated reach, and it belongs to you, as long as the number does.

This is why, when chat apps went quiet, phones in businesses across the country rang more than usual. Customers fell back to the channel that always works.

## But a phone line that nobody answers is not a channel

Here is the uncomfortable part. Many businesses moved to chat apps in the first place because their phone was not working well. Not technically, but practically.

The phone rang during the busiest hour and nobody could pick up. It rang after closing and nobody was there. It rang on the owner's personal mobile while he was driving. It rang one handset on one desk while three customers were standing at the counter. Customers learned that messaging got an answer and calling did not, and they changed their habits.

So if the phone is going to be the channel you control, it has to actually work. That means three things.

### 1. The number belongs to the business

In many small businesses the "business number" is the owner's own SIM. That works until the owner is on holiday, falls ill, sells the business or hands it to the next generation. A business number should be in the business's name, able to ring whoever is on duty, and able to stay with the business if any one person leaves. We wrote more about this in [choosing a business phone number in Nepal](/blog/business-phone-number-nepal).

### 2. It rings more than one person

A number that rings one handset fails whenever that one person is busy. Running the number through a [cloud phone system](/products/phone-system) lets the same number ring a team, ring staff on the app on their own phones, follow your opening hours, and hold callers in a short queue instead of giving them a busy tone. If you are still on an office PBX box, our guide on [whether to replace it](/blog/ip-pbx-vs-phone-line) walks through the choice.

### 3. Something answers when nobody can

This is the part most businesses skip, and it is the most important. A call at 9 pm, or the fifth call during the lunch rush, should not simply ring out. It should go to something that can help. A voicemail that someone actually checks. A callback list. Or an [AI call agent](/products/ai-call-agent) that answers in Nepali, handles the common questions, takes the booking or the order, and passes anything difficult to a person with a summary.

We have written about how to [measure what your phone is losing](/blog/measure-missed-calls-business). Most owners who count are surprised by how many calls go unanswered. Fixing that does more for customer contact than any app.

## What happens to calls if the internet goes down?

A fair question. If the business runs its phone line through an online phone system, does that not make the phone depend on the internet too?

Partly, and it is worth being honest about which part.

On the **caller's** side, nothing changes. Customers dial an ordinary number from an ordinary phone. Their call needs no data.

On the **business's** side, a cloud phone system runs on servers, not in the office. If the office internet goes down, the system is still up. It can still answer with the AI agent, still play a message, still queue calls, and, crucially, staff can **answer from the app on their own phones**, over mobile data, wherever they are. The office connection is not in the path.

That is a large improvement over the old office PBX, which sat in a cupboard and stopped answering entirely when the power or the line in that one building failed.

The setting worth checking is simple. Ask your provider: if our office internet is down, where do calls go? The right answer is "they still get answered: by staff on the app, or by the agent".

## Building contact you control: a plan for any business

None of this requires dropping chat apps. It requires putting them in the right place. Here is the structure we recommend.

**Make one phone number your main channel.** One number, owned by the business, printed on everything: signboard, receipts, website, Google listing, social profiles, vehicle, packaging. When customers are unsure how to reach you, this is what they should find first.

**Make that number reliable.** Ring a team, not a handset. Set opening hours. Give it a queue, and an answer when nobody is free. Record calls so you can check what was said, and tell callers you do, as covered in [recording business calls in Nepal](/blog/call-recording-consent-nepal).

**Keep chat apps as extra doors, not the front door.** WhatsApp, Viber, Instagram and Messenger are all welcome. List them as additional ways to reach you. But never let a customer's only record of your business be a chat thread.

**Keep your customer list somewhere you own.** If your only list of customers is the contact list inside a chat app, you lose it when you lose the app. Keep names, numbers and order history in your own system, so you can reach customers through any channel, including a plain phone call or SMS.

**Put your own website chat on your own website.** A [chatbot that answers from your own information](/products/website-chatbot), on a website you control, does much of what a chat app does for customers who find you online, and it cannot be blocked by anyone else's rules.

**Write a one-page plan for when a channel fails.** It does not need to be complicated. Which channel is primary, who checks each one, and what you tell customers if one goes down. "If WhatsApp is not working, call us on 01-XXXXXXX" posted on your signboard, website and Google listing the same morning is worth a great deal.

## For larger businesses: the same idea, bigger stakes

For banks, cooperatives, hospitals, internet providers and schools, the stakes are higher, because the people trying to reach you may need help urgently, and a regulator may ask how you serve them.

A cooperative whose members were told to "message us on Viber" with a payment question has a real problem when that does not work for a member. An internet provider whose fault reporting runs only through a Facebook page is unreachable at the exact moment a customer's internet is down. They are the one group who cannot reach Facebook.

For these organisations, the phone line is not just a backup. It is the channel that must always work, for everyone, including people without smartphones. It should be built and measured like core infrastructure: enough capacity for the peak, answers out of hours, and reports on how many calls were answered and how fast. ## A quick self-check

Answer these honestly:

- If WhatsApp stopped working at 10 am tomorrow, how would a customer with an open order reach us?
- Is our main business number in the business's name, or in one person's?
- When that number rings and the person who usually answers is busy, what happens?
- When it rings at 9 pm, what does the caller hear?
- Is our full customer list stored somewhere we own, outside any chat app?
- If the office internet goes down, where do our calls go?

If any answer is "I'm not sure", that is the thing to fix first.
