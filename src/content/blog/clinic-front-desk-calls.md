At nine in the morning, the OPD counter at most clinics in Kathmandu looks the same. A queue of patients holding blue files. A receptionist checking names, taking fees and printing tokens. And a phone on the desk that has started ringing and will not stop until noon.

The receptionist knows what most of those calls are about. Is the skin doctor sitting today? Can my father come on Wednesday instead? What time does the lab close? Each one takes a minute. With twelve people standing in front of her, a minute is something she does not have. So the phone rings out, or it gets picked up, put down on the desk, and forgotten.

On the other end is someone who has already decided to come to your clinic. They just needed one answer first.

## Why clinic calls are different

A shop that misses a call loses a sale. A clinic that misses a call loses something harder to see.

Patients rarely call a clinic for fun. They call because something is wrong, or because they are trying to fit a doctor's visit around work, school runs and the bus timetable. When the line is busy they do not leave a message. They try a different hospital, or they turn up anyway and wait two hours for a doctor who was never sitting that day.

Clinic calls also follow a pattern that makes them easy to answer and hard to keep up with. Almost all of them are about the same few things:

When a doctor sits. Which days, which hours, whether the visiting specialist still comes on Fridays.

Booking and moving appointments. A slot this week, a different day because the child has exams, a later time because the office will not let them leave.

Practical questions. Fees, the lab's opening hours, where to park, whether to come fasting for a blood test.

Reports and results. Whether the report is ready, and sometimes what it means.

The first three have answers that sit in the rota, the fee list and a few notes. The fourth one does not, and it should never be answered by anyone except your staff. That split is the whole idea.

## Answering the questions that have one right answer

An [AI call agent](/products/ai-call-agent) answers your clinic's normal number. It speaks Nepali, and it understands the way patients actually talk on the phone, with English words for tests and departments mixed in.

You give it the things your receptionist already uses: the doctor rota, OPD days, fees, lab hours and the answers to the questions people ask every day. When a patient asks if Dr. Shrestha is sitting on Wednesday, it checks the rota and says so. When they want that slot, it takes their name, books it and sends an SMS with the time.

When the rota changes, you change it once. The next caller hears the new schedule. There is nothing to re-record.

Because it can hold [up to fifteen calls at once](/products/ai-call-agent), the nine o'clock rush stops being a wall of engaged tones. Every caller gets through, at the moment your desk is busiest.

## The empty slot nobody talks about

Every clinic knows the other side of the problem. A patient books, then simply does not come. The doctor sits with a gap in the morning, and someone who needed that slot was told there were none.

Reminder calls fix a lot of this, and they are exactly the kind of job nobody at the front desk has time for. With the [AI phone system](/products/ai-phone-system), the day before each appointment the agent calls the patient, reminds them of the time and the doctor, and asks if they are still coming. If they say no, it can offer another day. Each answer is saved, so in the morning your desk can see which slots are likely to open up and who is on the waiting list.

It is a small change. Over a month it adds up to a lot of appointments that would otherwise have gone empty.

## Where the agent must stop

Healthcare is the one place where knowing what not to answer matters more than knowing what to answer.

The agent does not give medical advice. If a caller describes symptoms, asks whether a report is normal, or wants to know which medicine to take, it does not guess. It says that a member of staff will help and transfers the call, along with a short note of what the patient asked, so they do not have to repeat themselves.

If a caller describes an emergency, it tells them to contact emergency services or come in immediately, and puts them through to a person. It never tries to handle an emergency on its own.

You decide where these lines sit. Some clinics send every billing question to a person as well. Others let the agent explain the fee list and only hand over disputes. Either way, the rule is written once and followed on every call.

## What it changes for the people at the desk

The receptionist does not disappear. The difference is what she spends her morning on.

Instead of answering "is the doctor sitting today" forty times, she deals with the patient in front of her, the worried parent on the line who needs a real person, and the report that came back and needs a doctor's call. The phone questions that have one right answer are already handled, and every one of them is recorded and written out, so she can check what a caller was told if they come in confused.

For the patient, it simply means the phone gets answered.

## Where to start

Most clinics begin with the busiest hours and evenings, when the desk is either swamped or closed, and widen it once they have listened to a few weeks of calls. You keep your existing number, so patients notice nothing except that someone picks up.
