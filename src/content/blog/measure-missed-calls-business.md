A customer who walks into your shop and finds nobody at the counter is a missed sale you can see. You notice it, and you remember it.

A missed phone call leaves nothing behind. The phone rings, nobody picks up, and it stops. There is no empty chair, no complaint, no note. The caller simply rings the next number on the list. Most businesses have no idea how many of these they have, and so they cannot tell whether it is a small leak or a large one.

This guide shows how to find out. It needs a notebook or a spreadsheet and about a week. You do not need any software and you do not need to change your phone system to do it.

## Why you cannot just feel it

Ask a shop owner how many calls they miss and they will usually say "a few". Ask them to write down every call for a week and the answer is often higher, sometimes much higher.

There are good reasons for the gap. You remember the calls you answered, because they led to something. You forget the ones you missed, because nothing followed. A call missed at lunch, one missed while serving a customer, and one that came in after closing all feel like different kinds of event, so they never get added together.

Counting turns a feeling into a number. The number is what lets you decide whether to do something about it.

## What counts as a missed call

Be strict, or the count will flatter you. Four kinds of calls are lost:

**Rang out.** The phone rang and nobody picked up. This is the obvious one, and it shows in the call log.

**Answered, then dropped.** Someone picked up, said "hold on", put the phone down and never came back. The line shows as answered, but the caller experienced a miss.

**Busy.** The caller got an engaged tone because the line was already in use. These are the hardest to see, because many handsets do not log them at all.

**Outside hours.** Calls that came in after closing or on a holiday. You may think of these as fair, but the caller does not. They wanted an answer and went elsewhere.

Count all four. You can separate them later and decide which ones you care about.

## Collect a week of calls

Take seven days, including a weekend if you are open. Make a simple sheet with these columns:

| Time | Number | Answered? | Who answered | Call back? | Called back when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Fill it from three places:

1. **The handset's call history.** Most phones keep a list of missed and received calls. Copy it at the end of each day, because the list fills up and drops old entries.
2. **Your provider's call report.** If you use a business line or an exchange, ask for the call detail records for the week. These list every call that reached your number, including ones that never appeared on a handset. For busy tones, this is the only reliable source.
3. **A tally by hand.** Put a sheet next to the phone and have whoever answers add a mark. It is crude, but it catches the "answered and dropped" calls that nothing else does.

Do not try to be perfect. A rough week of real data is worth more than a careful plan that never starts.

## Work out four numbers

Once you have the sheet, four numbers tell you most of what you need.

**1. Total calls and missed calls.** Add them up. Missed divided by total gives your missed-call rate.

**2. Missed calls by hour.** Group the misses by hour of the day. Almost every business finds that the misses are not spread out. They bunch up at opening, at lunch, at the end of the day, or whenever the staff are busy with customers in front of them.

**3. Return rate.** Of the calls you missed, how many did you ring back? This is where the real damage hides. A missed call that is returned within ten minutes costs you little. One that is never returned is a lost customer.

**4. Time to return.** For the calls you did return, how long did it take? Minutes and hours are very different outcomes.

## What the pattern usually tells you

The shape of the numbers points at the cause.

Misses clustered around the same two or three hours usually mean you are short of hands at that time, not that you have too few people overall. The fix may be a rota change, not more staff.

Misses on weekends or after closing mean your hours do not match when people actually call. You do not have to be open, but you should know what you are missing.

A high miss rate with a low return rate is the worst combination. It means enquiries arrive, sit on the handset and go cold. Here the cheapest fix is often a simple habit: one person owns the missed-call list every hour.

A low miss rate with long return times means you answer most calls but the ones you miss wait too long. A shared list and a rule of "return within thirty minutes" often solves it.

## Put a rupee figure on it

Counting is only useful if it turns into money, because that is what makes a decision easy. The sum is short:

**missed calls per week × share that were new enquiries × share that would have bought × average sale**

Here is a worked example with made-up numbers, so you can see the shape of it. Use your own.

- You miss 30 calls a week.
- About two thirds are new enquiries, not existing customers: 20.
- Of 20 new enquiries, perhaps 1 in 4 would have bought: 5.
- An average sale is Rs 6,000.

That is about Rs 30,000 a week, or Rs 120,000 a month, in sales that never reached you. Even if you halve every assumption, the figure is still worth a conversation. If you cut the assumptions to a tenth and the figure is still small, then you have good news: your phone is not your problem.

The point is not accuracy. It is to find out whether the leak is the size of a cup or a bucket.

## Fixes that cost almost nothing

Before spending anything, try the cheap things.

**Give the phone an owner.** A phone that everyone is supposed to answer is a phone that nobody answers. Name a person for each part of the day.

**Keep a missed-call list and clear it.** One list, one person, cleared every hour. A missed call that is returned within minutes is mostly recovered.

**Put your hours and your most common answers where callers will find them.** A short message on the line, your opening hours on the signboard, or a note on your Google listing removes many calls that did not need to be calls at all.

**Plan for the busy hours.** If the misses cluster at lunchtime, schedule your phone person for lunchtime, and move other tasks.

**Track it again.** Run the same week after a month and compare. A number that falls is your proof that something worked.

## When counting is not enough

Some businesses find that even after tidying all of this up, the misses stay. The calls simply outnumber the people. Calls arrive in bursts, several at once, and one person cannot take them all. At that point you are choosing between more staff, a different way of handling calls, or accepting the loss. Your numbers tell you how big that loss is, which is what makes the choice an informed one.

Whichever you pick, you now have a figure and a method to check it against. That is more than most businesses ever have about their phone.
